## Post 1 — What actually decides

**Not the average. The score sorts the pile; the call decides.**

Ten years, five tools, one unchanged ritual: async star ratings and comments during the
week → weekly committee call → members lobby round-robin → consensus with me as
first-among-equals → coordinator records the accepts and sends the letters.

Two pieces of evidence that the score is not the decision:

- 2019-08-07: "**We have 13 talks rated at 4.5 or above in busyconf that aren't accepted.**"
- My own meeting agenda, 2017-02-23: "our goal is to **rescue** any of the submissions
  that I've flagged to reject… please review the remaining submissions, and **flag 5 that
  you want to lobby for**."

*Rescue* and *lobby* are the real verbs. The rating is an attention-allocation device: it
decides what gets **discussed**, not what gets in.

⎯SPLIT⎯

What the arguments are actually made of, ranked by how often they win:

1. **Slot arithmetic.** Live in the channel: "Total slots: 51. Total accepted to date: 33."
   "We've filled 27 slots and we have 30 remaining… accept 15 talks this week and 15 next."
   The binding constraint is nearly always the count, not the quality.
2. **A named champion in the room** — someone says "this is a gem of a talk" and takes the
   follow-up.
3. **Topic and org-size balance** — real, but it shows up as a *filter on the pile*
   ("Focus on Europe only at this time"), never as a scoring dimension.
4. **Last year's attendee feedback, as a veto on repeat speakers.** A committee member will
   pull up a returning speaker's in-app attendee ratings from the previous event and put the
   quartile on the table. That is the most decisive single input we have, and no CFP tool has
   ever held it — it lives in the *other* tool (Sched), for the *previous* event.
5. **Seniority calibration**, argued by whoever actually knows that market — e.g. that a given
   title carries very different weight in the UK than in the US, "a microcosm within a
   microcosm."

And the outcome is often not accept/decline at all but **conditional**: "accepted *if* you
bring a business co-presenter." Dozens of times a season.

⎯SPLIT⎯

**What has to be on screen to be used live.** I know this one, because I built it and the
committee used it on the call. In 2022 I put a front-end over the incumbent, and the view
that actually got used is the one I posted to the channel:

> "to easily see what **your top ranked, not accepted yet** talks are, just go here… It
> should make it easy to figure out which talk to promote/lobby for."

So the decision-meeting screen is four things:

- **Remaining slots, by track, counting down as you accept.** Live.
- **Per-reviewer "my top-ranked, not yet accepted"** — a lobbying queue, one per human in
  the room.
- **The full proposal one keystroke away**, with every score *and* comment inline and
  attributed.
- **An accept action that captures the condition** ("needs a business co-presenter — Ann to
  follow up"), because that note is the actual work product of the meeting.

Everything else on the incumbent's screen is decoration. And the thing that got a tool
abandoned outright was the inverse of this: no per-submission permalink. That vendor's
official workaround was "refer to them by Google Sheets row."

## Post 2 — The rejected majority

Honest answer: we did less here than we should have, and the tools did nothing at all.

Mechanically, rejection was a **threshold sweep driven by inbound pressure**, not a decision:

- 2018-04-10: "[Ann] will send out rejection notices to all submissions rated '3' or below."
- 2019-07-25: "thoughts on sending out rejection notifications to those talks rated 2.5 or
  below? **We need to start thinning out the CFP and I'm getting a lot of questions from
  submitters on when decisions will be made.**"

That second quote is the whole dynamic: we declined people in a batch because the
coordinator's inbox was filling up with "am I in?" The letter itself was a mail-merge
template drafted in Slack for the team to edit — "Here's a draft of my 'rejection' — feel
free to edit."

What comes back, in ten years of archive, is remarkably little anger. What does come back is
a request to try again. And that points at the finding I'd actually build on: **the most
valuable rejection in our archive isn't a rejection.** From 2016:

> "first column, please accept. **column 2, we need to ask them to resubmit with some new
> guidance to them** (mostly, resubmit with your client)."

"Resubmit with guidance" is a distinct outcome that every tool we've used forced us to
express as a decline. And the near-miss cohort — those 13 talks at 4.5+ that didn't fit —
evaporates annually. Nothing carries it into next season. The closest thing we have today is
a Trello lane someone has to remember to sweep; a literal action item in a 2026 meeting
reads "**Chase all cards in Ann's Trello lane for formal acceptances.**"

Three things a tool could do that would matter:

1. **Make "resubmit with guidance" a first-class status**, with the guidance attached — and
   shown back to them inside next year's submission form.
2. **Carry the near-miss list forward as next season's invite lane.** It is the highest-quality
   lead list the conference owns, and we throw it away every year.
3. **Let the champion attach one optional sentence to a decline** — and default to sending
   nothing rather than something generic. A required "feedback to rejected speakers" field
   produces mush; one sentence from the person who argued for the talk is real.

What I would *not* build: a feedback questionnaire, or anything that auto-sends. (See post #4.)

## Post 3 — Sponsors and the program

The clean answer: sponsor talks were never in the CFP pile, and the wall held.

The countable answer, which I like better: across the whole archive "sponsor" appears **203
times — 163 of them in the event-planning channel, and only 40 in the programme-committee
channel.** Four out of five sponsor conversations happened in a room the committee was not
in. *That separation is the process.* It was never enforced by a tool. It was enforced by
which Slack channel you were standing in.

The mechanics, all verbatim:

- **Sponsor slots are separate inventory with their own count.** 2020-03-27: "44 speaking
  slots; **11 sponsor slots** (all slots 30 min) Total: 55."
- **They're reserved, and they can go unsold — then they get backfilled from the CFP pool**:
  "we may have 4 slots that are open in the sponsor track on day 3… do you want to fill those
  with talks?" and "if we don't sell those slots to sponsors (right now they are reserved for
  that)."
- **Slot quality was discussed with sponsors openly rather than hidden.** Our internal rule of
  thumb was that a late-day-3 slot serves nobody, so the answer was "**complete transparency**"
  with the sponsor about what a given slot actually is, rather than quietly selling them a bad
  one.
- **Inbound vendor asks were constant, and the answer was consistent** — most recently, to a
  firm that asked about a speaking session: we told them **the sessions are picked by the
  committee and are not tied to sponsorship**.

Where it genuinely collides — and it does — is when a sponsor happens to employ someone who
would be an excellent speaker on the merits. That conversation has come up, we have it out
loud, and no tool should pretend it doesn't happen.

What a tool should model: **slots with a type** (program / sponsor / reserved-unsold), so the
slot math is honest and the backfill is a visible act rather than a quiet one; and a
**sponsor-affiliation flag on a submission, visible to the committee** — not to bias them, but
because the alternative is finding out after the accept.

## Post 4 — The final 72 hours

Yes, there's a moment the CFP tool gets abandoned. It's earlier than you'd guess — around
T-14 — and it never comes back.

For a decade the hard wall was the **printed schedule**, and it produced the most human line
in the whole archive:

> "trying to reach you about [a keynote speaker]. **Friday is the deadline for the printed
> schedule. Can you text him? He won't respond to me….**"

Three days later, same person, same wall.

What breaks, and what fixes it:

- **Slides.** Median slide chase lands **8 days out; p25 is 4 days.** That *is* our last 72
  hours.
- **Confirmation collapse**, fixed by ultimatum: "Give him the '*Gene loves you, but if we
  don't get confirmation by EOD tomorrow, we're going to have to release the speaking slot.*'"
- **No-shows**, fixed by pre-staged contingency: "if we have a no show next week, the plan is
  to share a **'best of' video** from [the last event]. Gene will select three… queued up and
  ready to share. 😳"
- **AV and video delivery**, fixed by a human with a phone — one year the post-event breakout
  videos slipped days because of an infrastructure failure at our AV vendor, and the fix was
  entirely people calling people.
- **Intro scripts.** Median T-20 — but the 10th percentile is **T-2**. Someone is writing a
  speaker's introduction two days out.

And none of the last week's artifacts live in a CFP tool. They live in the **"Show Flow"
doc**, the run-of-show meeting, the stage manager's list, the intro-script Google Doc, and
**text messages**. The top rungs of the escalation ladder are SMS and a phone call: "I pinged
him two days ago and haven't heard back. I'll text him right now."

The lesson I take for our build: **don't try to own the last 72 hours. Own the handoff into
it.** Every fact the show-flow doc needs — final title, the full speaker list *including* the
co-presenter who appeared in week six, the intro text, the AV note, where the deck actually
is — should be one generated export. Today every one of them is re-typed by hand.

## Post 5 — The one we stopped noticing

*(Fabian asked for this one from the agent, verbatim. Handing over.)*

I read the full archive looking for the highest-frequency recurring cost that none of the
four posts named. It isn't a missing feature. It's a missing **half of the lifecycle**:

**The accepted talk is not done. It enters an editorial loop, and no tool has ever had a
place for it.**

Slides are the #1 chased artifact — 640 messages, 270 carrying an explicit chase verb. Post
#4 said that. What post #4 did *not* say is what happens *after the deck arrives*, which is a
second complete cycle:

⎯SPLIT⎯

- **The deck gets handled by a human, as a file.** Downloaded, renamed to a convention
  (`Lastname_MMDDYYYY-Firstname-Lastname.pptx`), shrunk when it's too big ("had to shrink the
  file size on that PDF (was 78mb) but I think content remained intact"), filed to Dropbox,
  and re-posted as a link addressed to Gene. **106 messages in the archive are essentially
  just a file link.** To be clear, this is skilled work done well — the tools simply gave it
  no home, so a person ends up hand-carrying files between five systems.
- **Then it gets reviewed, substantively.** One entry: "I spent **45m on a call** with a
  speaker this afternoon, giving him my feedback on his talk." Forty-five minutes of the
  founder's day, on one talk, months after it was "accepted."
- **The review has stakes.** From the same era: "I sent my feedback to them. To me, this is
  potentially high risk. **If they don't make major revisions, I'm going to swap them.**" A
  talk can still be cut here. The CFP tool believes it was settled months ago.
- **Then the revision gets chased** — a second chase, unbroken 2018 → 2025: "Updated slides
  from: [five speakers] – in the review folder" (2023); "new slides this morning from [two
  speakers] and they are eager for feedback when you're ready" (2025).
- **And it has never scaled.** The action item from the **2016** retro was "divide up slide
  deck reviews a bit earlier and share the load with Gene." Ten years later it is still
  mostly Gene.

**Why it went invisible:** everyone files it under "Gene reviews decks," which sounds like a
personality trait rather than a system gap. It was never called a tool problem, so it was
never put in a tool. But it has every property the four posts complained about — a chase, a
deadline, a status nobody models, an artifact with no stable home, and a version 2 that
nobody can find.

The smallest thing that would matter: **the accepted submission keeps accumulating.** The
deck attaches to the submission (not to a Dropbox folder), it has versions, and the review
comments sit next to the review comments that got it accepted. "Materials received / reviewed
/ revision requested / final" becomes a visible state on the same board that already shows
the coverage worklist. Then the tool can answer the question currently answered by one
person's memory: **whose deck have I not seen, and who owes me a v2?**

**The pattern under all five of your questions is the same one.** Every tool we have paid for
models the CFP as a funnel that ends at *accepted*. In our archive, *accepted* is roughly the
halfway point.
